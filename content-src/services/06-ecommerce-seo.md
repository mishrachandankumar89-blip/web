## Why stores need their own kind of SEO

> A store with 5,000 products can easily have 500,000 URLs once filters, sorting and tracking parameters multiply.

Most of those URLs should never be indexed. Left alone, they eat crawl attention, split ranking signals across near-duplicates and bury the category pages that actually earn sales. At the same time, the pages that should rank are often thin: a manufacturer description copied across a hundred other shops, a title that only says the product code, and no answer to the questions shoppers ask.

E-commerce SEO fixes both sides. It controls what search engines see, and it makes what they see worth ranking.

Speed and friction matter as much as rankings. Baymard Institute's running average across 50 studies puts online cart abandonment at about 70 percent. Not all of that is fixable, but slow pages, surprise costs and long checkouts are. Our [Conversion Rate Optimization](service:conversion-rate-optimization) service picks up where SEO ends.

## Where e-commerce SEO wins

### Category pages first

Category pages target the broad, high-value searches: "men's running shoes", "cotton kurtis for office", "4K monitors under 30000". They usually deserve more SEO attention than individual products. We map searches to categories, add helpful introductory and buying-guide content, and make sure filters that match real searches, such as a colour or brand, get indexable landing pages while the rest stay out of the index.

### Product pages that answer questions

Unique descriptions, specifications in a clear table, sizing, materials, care, delivery times, return terms and real customer questions. We write templates that scale across thousands of products and hand-write the top sellers.

### Structured data and Merchant Center

Product markup with price, availability, ratings and shipping details helps Google show rich results. Connecting a clean product feed to Google Merchant Center makes your products eligible for free listings across Google surfaces, including the Shopping tab. We set up both and keep them in sync.

### Crawl and index control

Canonical tags for variants, rules for faceted navigation, pagination handling, out-of-stock and discontinued product policies, and XML sitemaps that include only the URLs you want indexed.

| Store problem | Effect | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Filter URLs indexed | Thousands of thin duplicates | Index only filters that match real searches; noindex or block the rest |
| Copied manufacturer text | Pages look identical to other shops | Unique descriptions and spec tables, starting with top sellers |
| Discontinued products deleted | Lost links and 404 errors | Redirect to the closest product or category, or keep with alternatives |
| Variants on separate URLs | Signals split across colours and sizes | Canonical to the main product, or one page with selectors |
| No product markup | No price or rating in results | Product structured data and a Merchant Center feed |

## Questions about e-commerce SEO {wide alt}

^ FAQ

::: faq
? Should category pages have text?
Yes, when it helps shoppers. A short introduction above the products and a buying guide or FAQ below give search engines context and answer real questions. Walls of keyword text do not help anyone.

? What should we do with out-of-stock products?
If the product is coming back, keep the page live, show the expected date and suggest alternatives. If it is gone for good, redirect to the closest product or category, or keep the page with clear alternatives if it still earns links and traffic.

? Are Google Shopping free listings worth it?
Usually, yes. They cost nothing beyond a clean feed in Merchant Center and can bring buyers who are already comparing products. We set them up alongside any paid Shopping campaigns.

? How do you handle thousands of product descriptions?
We build templates that pull in real attributes and add useful, specific sentences, then hand-write the products that earn most. That balance keeps quality high without stalling for months.
:::
